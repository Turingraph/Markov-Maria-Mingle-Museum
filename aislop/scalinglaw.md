But there's a huge catch

Your current random-dating model will not automatically reproduce real urban scaling laws.

In fact, this could become a really interesting experiment.

Suppose every agent makes a fixed number of random connections per timestep:

k=constant.

Then total interactions are approximately:

I(N)≈Nk.

That's linear scaling:

I(N)∝N.

So simply making the city larger doesn't magically produce superlinear behavior.

You need some mechanism through which increasing N changes the interaction opportunities per individual.

For example:

k(N)∝Nα

would give:

I(N)=Nk(N)∝N1+α.

Now you've got a mechanism capable of producing superlinear scaling.

That's a very nice computational experiment.

One thing I'd be careful about

There is substantial debate about how universal or theoretically explanatory urban scaling laws actually are. For example, Shalizi's reanalysis argued that some apparent scaling relationships could be explained by alternative functional forms and other structural effects rather than uniquely establishing the proposed power laws.

arXiv

So your simulator shouldn't be designed to "prove urban scaling."

A much better scientific attitude would be:

Given a specified microscopic model, what scaling behavior does it generate, and which mechanisms cause the exponent to change?

That is experimentally much cleaner.

And honestly, this makes your original project idea more coherent than it might have seemed at first. Your dating/chemistry examples aren't necessarily the ultimate subject. They're convenient toy domains for testing a more general engine:

local rules → interaction network → temporal dynamics → emergent macroscopic statistics.

That same machinery can be used to investigate dating, chemical-style bonding, epidemics, cities, or other networked systems—without claiming that the toy model accurately represents any of them.
